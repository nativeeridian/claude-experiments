      Voice is the OS.

      I went to Lincoln Center for a voice company's product day and watched two things at once. One was a company pitching the operating layer for how we'll use software: you talk, it listens, and then it acts. The other was a masterclass in how to launch it. This is part recap, part case study, read with one question in mind: what does all of this mean if you work somewhere a regulator reads the transcripts?

      The case

      EL · 11.11 · NYC

      How to launch a platform in a concert hall

      The company
      ElevenLabs. Founded in 2022, valued at $11 billion, and best known to developers for lifelike AI voices.
      The challenge
      Graduate from developer favorite to enterprise infrastructure, and win over buyers who don't write code.
      The stage
      David Geffen Hall at Lincoln Center, home of the New York Philharmonic.
      The audience
      About 2,000 customers and partners, and for once, not just technologists.
      The play
      Live demos, plain language, customers on stage, booths to try partner agents, and a human moment.
      My verdict
      A marketing masterclass. The story runs ahead of what ships, but the launch itself didn't miss.

      01Side A · The showCase study

      The room is the product

      David Geffen Hall · Lincoln Center · home of the New York Philharmonic

      Start with the room. ElevenLabs could have rented a convention center or a hotel ballroom. Instead it held the summit at Lincoln Center, in David Geffen Hall, the home of the New York Philharmonic.

      If your company is betting that the future of computing runs on voices so lifelike you forget they're synthetic, you put that bet in a building engineered for the best sound in the city. The venue was the first demo, and nobody had to say so.

      2,000/2,200Attendees, in a concert hall with about 2,200 seats

      Geffen Hall reopened in 2022 after a roughly $550 million rebuild meant to fix acoustic problems it had carried since 1962. Even its wood panels are shaped after sound waves. For a company that sells sound, that isn't a backdrop. It's an argument.

      You don't tell people your voices sound real. You book the room where sound gets judged.

      It also says something about ambition. Developer conferences happen in hotel ballrooms. Launches you want people to remember happen in concert halls.

      02Side A · The show10:00 AM · Keynote

      No safety net

      Mati Staniszewski · Co-founder & CEO, ElevenLabs

      Anyone who has run an AI demo knows the fear. You can build it carefully and rehearse it all week, and it can still go off the rails in front of the room. These systems are probabilistic. Audiences aren't.

      Mati ran it live anyway, and it was flawless. There was no sizzle reel and no recording with a confident voiceover. It was the actual product, working in real time, in a hall built for orchestras. It had the energy of the great product keynotes, Steve Jobs doing a real demo, where the confidence comes from the fact that it's really happening.

      Live is the only demo that proves reliability. A recording only proves editing.

      Just as telling was the language. It wasn't overtly technical. The story came in outcomes a CFO or a head of customer experience could repeat on Monday, like this line:

      If AI is going to work for us, and alongside us, it will need to interact naturally and intuitively with people in many different settings.

      Mati Staniszewski, at the summit

      This felt like ElevenLabs arriving on a bigger stage. The company developers made famous was speaking past them, to the executives who sign enterprise contracts.

      03Side A · The showAll day · Exhibit floor

      Try it yourself

      Partner booths · including Bancolombia's Customer Support FAQ agent

      The exhibits outside the hall made the same argument in a different key: if you don't believe us, try it. Partners set up small booths with their own agents running live, and you could walk up and talk to them.

      Bancolombia was showing its customer support FAQ agent. You put on the headphones, held a button to talk, and asked it about the bank. It was impressive, and it was honest about where it is. The booth spelled out its scope: products and services, cards and loans, requirements and documentation, fees and benefits, and self-service help.

      One of the suggested questions was "How can I send money?" Today the agent is built to tell you how, not to do it for you. The bank is still working through the rollout, and the ambition to go agentic is clear but not there yet. That booth previewed the theme of the day's best session, the gap between answering and acting, which I come back to on Side B.

      Even the demo screen carried a line noting that the bank is supervised by Colombia's Financial Superintendency. In regulated finance, the regulator is in the room even at a trade-show booth.

      A keynote creates belief. A booth creates proof.

      From a marketing seat, the booths were the smartest money spent. A partner's own agent is proof that isn't ElevenLabs talking about itself. It's a customer vouching for the product with its own brand on the line.

      Not every exhibit was a product demo. One was a listening room lined with acoustic foam, where you put on headphones and heard New Yorkers' stories, captioned on a screen built like a giant speaker. It didn't pitch anything. It let the voices do the work, which is the company's whole argument in one room.

      04Side A · The showCase study

      The playbook

      What ElevenLabs sold, and how it sold it

      Underneath the showmanship was a real thesis. ElevenLabs wants to be the interaction layer: the software between people and every model they talk to. It listens, decides whose turn it is, picks which model should do the thinking, and answers in a voice you'd want to hear.

      Within a few years, conversational agents will become critical infrastructure for every enterprise, as essential as cloud or payments.

      Mati Staniszewski, opening keynote

      Coming from financial services, that last word stuck with me. Payments is the most supervised plumbing we run, and payments-grade infrastructure comes with payments-grade expectations.

      How they sold it is the part worth stealing. Four moves:

      1. Let the room make the argument.A company selling lifelike voice launched in the home of the New York Philharmonic.
      2. Demo live.Real product, real time, no recordings. It's the riskiest kind of demo and the only kind that proves reliability.
      3. Talk to the buyer, not the builder.Plain language and outcomes, so the room could hold executives, not just engineers.
      4. Let people try it.Partner booths with live agents, like Bancolombia's. Proof beats claims, as long as you're honest about the scope.

      The story still runs ahead of the product. Much of what was announced is preview or roadmap. Great marketing buys attention, and now ElevenLabs has to ship into it.

      05Side B · The day10:45 AM · Panel

      From answering to acting

      Carlos Coto, Chief Strategy & Innovation Officer, Customers Bank · Daniela Mineiro, Experience Leader, Inter · Douglas Hamilton, Head of AI Research & Engineering, Nasdaq · with Ethan Tandowsky, CFO, ElevenLabs

      2M+ / moCustomer service interactions at Banco Inter, in Portuguese, English and Spanish

      This was the session I came for, and it named the gap I'd seen at the Bancolombia booth. The first wave of voice agents answered questions. The next wave does things: it checks the balance, and then it moves the money.

      That one verb changes the risk profile completely. An agent that answers is a content problem: is it accurate, is it compliant, does it sound like us? An agent that acts is a controls problem. Who authorized this? How do we know who's calling? What's the limit, and where's the evidence? Banks already answer those questions for people and for APIs. Voice agents need the same answers before the first transfer, not after it.

      Full self-service for the ones that don't want to interact with humans, and human beings powered by agents for the people that value the interaction.

      Carlos Coto, Customers Bank, describing its ElevenLabs rollout

      That's the framing I'd hand to any regulated team. The human handoff is part of the product. Design it first, measure it, and make it fast.

      06Side B · The day11:55 AM · Panel

      The 360-degree cost

      Matt Yanchyshyn, VP, AWS · Yael Taqqu, Senior Partner, McKinsey · with Ashish Uchil, Global BD & Partnerships, ElevenLabs

      The session I've thought about most since was "What it takes to scale AI across the enterprise," and in particular a point Matt Yanchyshyn from AWS made that almost nobody talks about: the hidden costs of AI. He called it the 360-degree cost.

      AI can take the pressure off a support queue. But what happens when demand explodes? If you automate one step, who handles the next one your support team inherits? If demand generation is mostly automated and it works, do you have the people in place to handle everything it sends you?

      Automation doesn't remove work. It moves it, and sometimes it multiplies it.

      In a bank, this gets concrete fast. If a voice agent resolves most routine calls, the calls left for people are the hardest ones: disputes, fraud, complaints, the customer who's already upset. A rate change or a fraud event can spike volume overnight. The agent scales instantly. The back office, the reviewers and the complaint handlers don't. Budget for the whole system, not just the bot.

      90%+AI utilization at AWS, sustained for years, as Matt Yanchyshyn described it

      Choose the right tools, and obsess over the rest.

      The AWS philosophy, as Matt Yanchyshyn put it

      There's no shortage of shiny tools. Matt's point was that adoption comes from purpose, not from any particular tool, and AWS has learned that the specific tool matters less than you'd think.

      The shift that stuck with me is how AWS now thinks about engineers: as product engineers. With AI taking on so much of the typing, engineers are expected to bring a strategic lens and really understand the product. The goal is to generate product in a spec-driven way, not to generate stuff. That takes real skill in building the harness, the specs, tests and guardrails around the AI, instead of shipping cheap code with nothing holding it together.

      For regulated teams, that's good news. A spec is something an auditor can read. Spec-driven work already looks like the way model risk, audit and change control think, which makes it the shortest path to AI that passes review.

      Yael Taqqu from McKinsey widened the frame. This is a technology revolution, she said, and it's also a talent revolution. People have to be open to building new skills, and leaders need boldness and humility working together. It's a rare combination. Most leaders arrive with the boldness. The humility has to be learned.

      Bold enough to ship. Humble enough to scope it.

      I'd seen that combination on the exhibit floor. Bancolombia was bold enough to put a live agent in front of strangers, and humble enough to keep it to the questions it can answer well. That's the posture regulated industries need right now.

      08Side B · The day4:30 PM · Closing session

      Waste on the way to useful

      "A conversation on building an AI-first company" · Eric Glyman, Co-founder & CEO, Ramp · with Mati Staniszewski

      The day closed with Ramp's Eric Glyman in conversation with Mati, and it wasn't a tour of Ramp's product. It was about something harder: how a company actually captures the potential of AI.

      The idea I've repeated most since came from him: some AI waste is the price of AI usefulness. People need room to try the tools, burn some cycles and learn what works. The mistake is getting stuck in that phase instead of moving past it to purpose.

      You have to waste some AI to get to useful AI.

      In a bank, "waste" is a hard word to say out loud. But a sanctioned sandbox where people can experiment safely is how you get to useful, and it's far cheaper than the shadow AI that fills the gap when there isn't one.

      2+2=11What a chatbot might have told you in 2023, in Eric Glyman's example

      He also gave the best illustration of the day of how fast this is moving. In 2023, he said, ChatGPT might tell you 2 + 2 was 11, or miscount the r's in "strawberry." Today, OpenAI's most advanced models are solving math problems no mathematician had ever solved. That isn't hype: this year an internal OpenAI model disproved an 80-year-old Erdős conjecture, and outside mathematicians checked the proof.

      That's the case for governance that can keep up. The model a bank approves this quarter won't be the model it runs next year, and controls that assume the technology stands still will be out of date before they're signed. It was the right note to end the day on: an exciting time to be building, and no time to stand still.

      09Side CSo what

      Which model answered?

      The Interaction Engine is the most important thing ElevenLabs previewed, and the least flashy. It manages turn-taking and interruptions, and it routes each moment of a conversation to a different model: something cheap to triage, something frontier-grade for the hard part.

      That's great economics. It's also a new question for model risk. In a bank, "the AI said it" is not an answer. For every turn we'll need to know which model spoke, which version, what it was given, and why the router picked it.

      One customer call can now be answered by several models. Each of them belongs in the model inventory.

      The vendor that makes that lineage a standard export, per call and per turn, becomes the easiest one in the room to approve. If I were ElevenLabs, I'd ship that before Memory.

      10Side CSo what

      What I'm taking away

      Six things I'm taking away, and would put in front of any risk committee looking at voice agents this year.

      1. Govern voice agents as a channel.Hold them to the same bar as a branch or the app: disclosures, complaints handling, QA sampling and accessibility.
      2. Retire voice as a credential.Keep voice as the interface. Verify identity with factors a clone can't copy.
      3. Start where acting has hard limits.Pick high-volume flows with firm thresholds and a fast human handoff, then widen the scope on evidence.
      4. Demand per-turn lineage.Transcript, model, version, action and reason, exportable for every call.
      5. Treat memory and training data as records.Design retention, consent and deletion on day one, not after the first exam finding.
      6. Budget for the 360.Plan for what automation pushes downstream: the hard calls, the demand spikes, and the people and reviewers behind the agent.

      ∞Side D · Hidden track4:00 PM · Impact Program

      Two voices, returned

      The moment that stayed with me had nothing to do with banking. In the Impact Program session, led by ElevenLabs' Gabi Leibowitz, Greg Kelner and Abdi Omar, who both lost their voices to illness, performed on stage using voices they rebuilt with ElevenLabs.

      ElevenLabs used the moment to widen its Impact Program: free, lifetime voice restoration for people with permanent voice loss, free ElevenMusic for songwriters through Songwriters of North America, a partnership with the Wounded Warrior Project, and a path for patients at NYU Langone's Rusk Rehabilitation.

      The same technology that makes a voice forgeable gave two people theirs back.

      Both things are true. That's exactly why the industries I care about need to get the controls right, so that we get to keep the good part.
