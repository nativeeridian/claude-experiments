Question one

“What is ASR?”

ASR is automatic speech recognition: the part of a voice agent that turns what you say into text. It's the agent's ears. ElevenLabs sells its own, called Scribe, and launched Scribe v3 that same day.

So we'd heard plenty about Scribe. As far as I heard, nobody on stage said “ASR” all day. The first thing standing between a room of AI-curious people and a free piece of merch was a vocabulary test in industry jargon.

People froze, guessed, asked it to repeat itself, and tried again. Eventually the staff standing nearby started helping people through it. The humans were in the loop the whole time. They just weren't part of the design.

The agent needed ASR to hear you say “ASR.”

Question two was easier, so easy I can't remember it. Question three asked which company has the best voice models. And that was it. Three spoken questions, plus the retries, multiplied by every person in the line, added up to well over an hour.

The point

AI can add friction, even when it's built to remove it.

Every part of that booth was designed to take friction out. No staff to wait on, no forms: just talk. As far as I could tell, nothing broke. The voice agents did what they were set up to do. And it still produced a wait of well over an hour.

The friction didn't disappear. It moved, out of the conversation and into everything around it.

01Into the queueEvery second the conversation added was multiplied by everyone standing behind it.
02Into the unknownFrom the line, nobody could see the booths, how long the wait was, or why. The answer was around a corner, behind a long wall.
03Into peopleStaff who weren't part of the design ended up helping anyway, one stuck question at a time.

Why I care about this in regulated industries

Strip out the merch and the booth is a pattern I see in financial services all the time: a voice agent placed in front of something a customer wants, with questions that serve the process more than the person.

Design for the whole journey, not just the conversation

As more of these agents end up at the front door, the job changes. It's no longer just making one conversation smooth. It's deciding where friction belongs across the whole experience.

  1. Design the journey, not just the dialogue.The conversation was smooth. The hour around it wasn't. Map the line, the wait, the signage and the fallback, not only the script.
  2. Make the wait visible.We couldn't see the booths, the time or the reason. Telling people what's ahead, and why, changes how a wait feels.
  3. Ask only what the moment needs.The booth needed to know which item you wanted. Everything after that felt like a quiz.
  4. Speak your customer's language, not your roadmap's.“ASR” is how builders talk. Customers say “it didn't hear me.”
  5. Put friction only where it protects someone.Banks add friction on purpose: fraud checks, step-up verification, cooling-off periods. Good friction protects a customer. Accidental friction just slows everyone down.

The part I keep thinking about

Don't let the first human conversation be the worst one.

At the booth, the humans only showed up once someone was stuck. They stepped in at the most flustered moment, in front of a line, to help with a question nobody should have been asked.

That's a small version of something much bigger. Design a simple automation well enough and you can confidently take people out of the process. The agent handles the easy things. Then a complicated matter comes along that it can't handle, and it goes to somebody else.

Now the first time a client ever speaks to a person is during that complicated issue, and they're already upset. They've fought the bot, they're repeating themselves to a stranger, and the relationship starts in a crisis.

Responsible design has to be considerate of what you want the human experience to be, and of how relationships get built. In financial services, the relationship is the product. Trust gets built in small, easy moments. If automation takes every easy moment, people only meet you on the hardest day.

This isn't hypothetical in banking. In 2023 the CFPB warned that chatbots can trap customers in “doom loops” with no path to a human, and that a chatbot has to meet the same consumer-protection obligations as the process it replaces. Read the CFPB's issue spotlight

What responsible design looks like

  1. Design the hand-off as the main event.Pass the whole conversation along so nobody has to repeat themselves, and have the person open by acknowledging what already happened. A warm hand-off is where trust is won or lost.
  2. Escalate on signals, not on failure.Frustration, repeated tries, money at risk or signs of vulnerability should bring in a person early. Nobody should have to fail three times to earn one.
  3. Spend the time automation saves on relationships.Use the hours the agent frees up for welcomes, check-ins and milestones, so the first human conversation isn't a complaint.
  4. Always leave a visible door to a person.Say up front what the agent can't do, and make “talk to someone” easy to find, not a reward for persistence.
  5. Measure the relationship, not just the deflection.Track how people feel after a hand-off, repeat contacts, and time to a human on hard issues. And watch the team that only takes escalations: a queue of nothing but upset people burns out good people.

The kicker

Friction doesn't disappear. It moves.

The summit was incredibly well run, and the technology worked. That's exactly why this hour stuck with me. Nothing failed. The friction came from choices made around the technology: what to ask, in what order, and what the people waiting could see.

As voice becomes the interface for more of what we do, the work isn't only removing friction from one conversation. It's designing the whole experience, and the relationship, on purpose: friction only where it protects someone, and a person there before the hardest day, not just on it.
